The Senate AI Committee Picks a Lane — and the Rest of the Agenda Waits

South Carolina's new Senate AI committee opened by limiting itself to AI inside state government, while campaign ads, data collection and a quiet House panel circled outside the room.

South Carolina's Senate Special Committee on Artificial Intelligence held its first meeting on Wednesday, Sept. 161, and the most consequential thing it did was draw a boundary: the committee will limit its work to how AI is incorporated into state government2. Almost everything else in the week's news ran wider than that boundary — a House panel looking at K-12, a Charleston industry forum on data and energy, AI in campaign ads. That gap between the breadth of the questions and the state's limited standing capacity to answer them is the real story.

A first meeting that starts with definitions

The published agenda set out three substantive items: the committee's scope of work, a survey of other states' AI definitions and task forces, and expert testimony3. Members met in Room 308 of the Gressette Building3. Chairman Tom Young of Aiken, who represents District 24, said the Senate should understand how AI is defined before it adopts permanent policies or regulatory frameworks4, and that AI's involvement in state government, education, law enforcement and other agencies is expected to grow5.

Legislative staff told members that 33 states have adopted AI definitions in law and that South Carolina is among the 17 that have not1. Bryant Walker Smith of the USC School of Law told the committee a definition may prove useful depending on how the process unfolds, but cautioned against defining terms without a clear purpose4. Homayoun Valafar, director of USC's AI Institute, urged lawmakers to write policy aimed specifically at generative AI and its appropriate use6. Sen. Josh Kimbrell of District 11 pressed on accountability — who remains legally responsible for what an AI system does later4 — and Sen. Matt Leber of Johns Island, also a committee member3, said constituents want lawmakers involved "before the toaster burns the house down"2.

Scope and timeline: what's settled, what isn't

Coverage of the meeting diverged. The Post and Courier reported the committee limited itself to AI in state government2, while broadcast reports described a broader multi-year examination touching education, law enforcement, and impacts on citizens and businesses56. Young's own comment cuts toward the narrower reading: he said the panel received emails from citizens and businesses in the 24 hours before the meeting and that many people misunderstood its scope2.

The timeline splits the same way. The committee is scheduled to run through the summer of 20287, with a final report and recommendations for state policymakers targeted by that point2; members said they hope to have recommendations ready roughly a year from now, ahead of the 2028 session7. The agenda itself set no deadline3. Read 2028 as the scheduled horizon and the earlier date as members' stated ambition.

The record the committee inherits

Of roughly 31 AI-related bills introduced at the State House last session, two became law — both on criminal justice matters, including computer-generated child sexual abuse imagery and non-consensual obscene AI-generated imagery2. (That count comes from SCAIO's own bill tracking, as cited by the Post and Courier.) Other bills stalled without much discussion, attributed to industry pushback or concern about constraining emerging technology2. The Council of State Governments places South Carolina behind most other states on AI legislation2, and the state has passed few AI-specific laws and has no comprehensive regulatory framework5.

The House side illustrates the capacity problem. A committee created by House leadership in late 2023 has met infrequently since its work was folded into the House Regulations Committee2. Its chairman, Rep. Jeff Bradley of Hilton Head, has been exploring how to include AI in South Carolina's K-12 curriculum2. Rep. Brandon Guffey of Rock Hill, a member, said he has grown frustrated with the panel's work amid fear of overregulating the technology2. House Majority Leader Micah Caskey of West Columbia said lawmakers handle AI reasonably well once a specific problem is identified, but that broader uses are harder to regulate2 — a fair description of why narrow criminal-justice bills passed and the rest did not.

Outside the State House

The South Carolina Emerging Tech Association hosted the Ascend SC forum in Charleston, bringing lawmakers and technology experts together on the state's economy and energy8. Rep. Greg Ford of Dorchester County, District 98, attended while drafting legislation8 and said lawmakers are working on a bill to limit what data government can collect about residents8. Gov. Henry McMaster signed Senate Bill 163, described as protecting digital rights, in May8. Sen. Kimbrell recently held a district town hall focused on data centers2.

Two adjacent gaps stayed open this week. No current South Carolina law requires disclosure of whether a user or a voice is AI2, and the state has no broad laws governing AI in political campaigns; bills to add election-cycle guardrails have not gained traction9. Coastal Carolina University political science professor Gibbs Knotts said disclosure is one option states are weighing9. Meanwhile, Microsoft data indicate South Carolina has relatively small shares of its workforce using AI compared with other states, concentrated in Richland, Charleston and Oconee counties2 — a reminder that adoption and rulemaking are both still early here.

On the public-understanding side, USC's AI Institute will host a campus workshop on Oct. 23 to help seniors with AI tools, digital literacy and online safety10. USC computer science professor Biplav Srivastava, who leads the AI for Society research group, described AI less as an autonomous threat than as an accelerator of human intent10.

Have something to tell the committee?

Public comment goes to AIcommittee@scsenate.gov

The Senate Special Committee on Artificial Intelligence accepts written comment by email3. Given that the chairman said many residents misread the panel's scope2, comments that speak directly to state government's own use of AI are most likely to land inside it.

What we're watching

Whether the Senate committee's state-government scope2 holds as public comment arrives2. Whether a statutory definition of AI emerges as a standalone bill, given that 33 states already have one1 and at least one witness warned against defining for its own sake4. Whether the House Regulations Committee takes AI up again2, and what Rep. Bradley's K-12 work produces2. Rep. Ford's government data-collection bill8. And the question none of this week's activity resolved: who builds the state's standing capacity to keep learning between now and summer 20287.
