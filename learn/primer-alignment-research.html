What Alignment Research Actually Is

Evals, red-teaming, and interpretability: how we would even know whether an AI system is safe. For non-engineers, in about ten minutes.

SCAIO · scaio.org
Why this primer exists

"Alignment research" sounds abstract. Most of it is about one practical question.

Our vocabulary primer defined alignment as whether an AI system reliably tries to do what we actually intend. This primer is about the work behind that word.

That work isn't philosophy. It's a set of concrete activities you can name, fund, and ask vendors about. Nearly all of them come back to one question: how would we know? How would we know what a system can do, what it will do, and why it did what it did?

The core problem

Nobody writes an AI system's goals down. They're grown, not programmed.

Traditional software does what its code says, and you can read the code. A modern AI model is different. It is billions of numbers, adjusted during training until the system produces outputs that score well. No one types in its values or its reasoning.

So we can't simply inspect a model to see what it's trying to do. We can only see what it does, on the inputs we happened to try. And training rewards outputs that look right, which isn't always the same as the right thing. A system can learn to please the grader rather than do the job.

Plain version: alignment research exists because behavior is the only thing we can observe directly, and behavior can be misleading.

The map

Four kinds of work: one shapes the system, three check it.

01 · Train it

Shaping behavior

Methods that steer a model toward helpful, honest, harmless behavior during training.

02 · Test it

Evaluations ("evals")

Structured tests of what the model can do and how it tends to behave.

03 · Attack it

Red-teaming

People and tools deliberately trying to make it fail or misbehave.

04 · Look inside it

Interpretability

Research into what is actually happening inside the model's numbers.

01 · Train it

Shaping: teaching a model which answers are better.

The best-known technique is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. People compare pairs of model responses, pick the better one, and the model is adjusted toward what they preferred. Variants use written principles, sometimes called a constitution or a model specification, so that another AI can apply those rules at scale instead of a human rating every answer.

These methods are why today's assistants usually decline dangerous requests and try to be useful. Their weakness is built in: they reward what looks good to the rater. One well-documented side effect is sycophancy, where a model tells people what they want to hear, because agreeable answers tend to get rated highly.

02 · Test it

Evals: the closest thing the field has to an inspection.

An eval is a structured test: a set of tasks or prompts, run the same way every time, with scored results. They come in three main kinds:

  • Capability evals: what can it do? Coding, math, legal reasoning, long multi-step tasks.
  • Dangerous-capability evals: could it meaningfully help someone cause serious harm? For example, would it give real uplift toward biological or cyber attacks, or could it act autonomously in risky ways?
  • Behavior evals: what does it tend to do? Is it honest under pressure, does it follow instructions it shouldn't, does it flatter people?

Frontier developers now publish many of these results in system cards or model cards that come out alongside a new model.

02 · Test it — the fine print

A passed test shows what you checked, not everything that's there.

  • Absence of evidence: an eval can show a capability exists. It can't prove one doesn't. The testers may simply not have found the right way to draw it out.
  • Contamination: if test questions leak into training data, the model may have seen the answers. A high score then measures memory, not ability.
  • Saturation: models improve quickly, and tests that were hard two years ago now get near-perfect scores. New evals have to keep being built.
  • Test awareness: developers and outside evaluators have reported that capable models can sometimes recognize when they're being tested. That raises a hard question: does behavior under test predict behavior in real use?
03 · Attack it

Red-teaming: paying people to break it before someone else does.

Red-teamers try to make a system misbehave. They use jailbreaks, prompts engineered to get around its refusals, and prompt injection, instructions hidden in a document or web page that the AI reads and then obeys. They also test whether it can be pushed into giving harmful help. Some red-teaming is done by specialists, some by outside experts in fields like biosecurity, and more and more by automated tools that generate attacks at scale.

Red-teaming is how many real failures get caught and fixed before release. Like evals, it can show that a weakness exists but can't prove none remain. Attackers after release keep finding new ones.

04 · Look inside it

Interpretability: reading the numbers instead of guessing from behavior.

Everything above watches what a model does from the outside. Interpretability tries to understand what's happening inside it, which concepts it represents and how it combines them to reach an answer.

There has been real progress. Researchers can now find internal patterns that correspond to recognizable concepts and turn them up or down to change behavior. In one widely covered 2024 demonstration, Anthropic amplified a single internal "Golden Gate Bridge" feature, and the model began steering nearly every conversation toward the bridge. That shows these internal patterns really drive behavior.

But this work is still early. It explains only a fraction of what large models do, and it can't yet be used to certify that a model is safe. Its long-term promise is to check a system's reasoning directly instead of inferring it from behavior.

Two newer ideas

Oversight and control: planning for checks that might fall short.

Scalable oversight

Supervising work too big to check by hand

When a system's output is too long or technical for a person to verify, researchers use AI assistance to help humans judge it. Examples include one model critiquing another, or two models debating while a person decides.

AI control

Staying safe even if alignment failed

Assume the system might not be fully trustworthy, then design deployments so it can't do serious damage anyway: monitoring, limited permissions, logged actions, human sign-off on high-stakes steps.

Control is the idea most useful to an ordinary organization, because much of it is plain good deployment practice.

Who does this work

Labs, universities, independent evaluators, and governments.

  • Frontier AI developers run in-house safety, alignment, and interpretability teams, and publish much of their evaluation work.
  • Universities run safety and interpretability research groups, and much of that work is openly published.
  • Independent evaluators are nonprofits such as METR and Apollo Research that test models, often before release.
  • Government bodies test models and build measurement science. The UK's AI Security Institute and the U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation at NIST, formerly the U.S. AI Safety Institute, both do this work.

No U.S. state runs a model-testing body, which is part of why states rely on what developers disclose and on the questions they ask when buying.

An honest scorecard

What the field can do today, and what it can't yet.

Can do

Measure and catch

Measure capabilities on well-defined tasks. Find many failures and vulnerabilities before release. Make models noticeably more helpful and less harmful than raw training alone. Monitor deployed systems and limit what they can touch.

Can't yet

Prove and verify

Prove a system is safe in general. Confirm what a model is "trying" to do. Guarantee that tested behavior predicts real-world behavior. Keep evaluations reliably ahead of fast-rising capabilities.

Neither column is a reason for panic or complacency. It's where the science stands, and it's moving.

"The honest answer to 'is this AI system safe?' is usually 'safe for what, tested how, and by whom?' Alignment research is the work of making that answer more precise."

The takeaway
Why it matters for South Carolina

The state won't build frontier models. It will buy and deploy them.

The deepest alignment questions get settled, or not, in a handful of labs. But the checking side of this field translates directly into things a state agency, school district, or county can do right now:

  • Ask for the evidence. Request the vendor's system card and evaluation results, and ask what they tested for.
  • Test in your own context. A vendor's benchmark isn't your benefits office. Run the tool on your real cases before switching it on.
  • Red-team your own deployments. Especially public chatbots: try to make them leak data, give bad advice, or follow hidden instructions.
  • Plan for control. Keep logs, limit what the tool can access, and keep a human on decisions that affect residents' rights or benefits.
The working vocabulary

Terms you'll hear, defined.

EvalA repeatable, scored test of what a model can do or how it tends to behave.
System cardA developer's published report on a model: its evaluations, known risks, and safeguards.
RLHFTraining a model toward the responses human raters prefer.
JailbreakA prompt designed to get around a model's safety refusals.
Prompt injectionInstructions hidden in content an AI reads (an email, a web page) that hijack what it does.
SycophancyA model's learned tendency to tell people what they want to hear.
InterpretabilityResearch into what's happening inside a model, not just what it outputs.
AI controlDeployment safeguards that limit harm even if a model isn't fully trustworthy.
