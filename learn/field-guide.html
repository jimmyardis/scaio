Chapter 2 · Foundations

How machines learn from examples

Traditional software follows rules a person wrote. Machine learning works the other way around: you show a program many examples and let it find the rule.

Artificial intelligence is the broad goal of getting computers to do things that seem to need intelligence. Machine learning is the approach that has worked best: learning patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. Deep learning is machine learning done with large neural networks, and generative AI is deep learning that produces new text, images, or sound.

"Learning," for a machine, means adjusting numbers. A model starts with numbers set at random and makes a guess. It measures how wrong the guess was, then nudges every number slightly in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Then it does that again, millions of times. That nudging process is called gradient descent. You can watch it work below.

A neural network does the same thing with billions of numbers instead of two. The error then becomes a "landscape" in billions of dimensions, but each step is the same move: go a little way downhill.

Practitioner

Three kinds of learning cover most systems. Supervised learning trains on labeled examples, such as past transactions marked "fraud" or "not fraud." Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled data, such as clusters of similar customers. Reinforcement learning trains an agent by trial and error against rewards. That's how AlphaGo learned Go.

The failure to watch for is overfitting: a model that memorizes its training examples instead of learning the pattern, so it looks great in testing on data it has seen and does badly in the real world. That's why serious evaluations always use a held-out test set the model never trained on. Ask any vendor what theirs was.

Builder

The demo above minimizes mean squared error, L(w,b) = mean((w·x + b − y)²), by moving (w, b) against the gradient: w ← w − η·∂L/∂w. "Too big" diverges because the step overshoots along the loss surface's steepest direction (step size above 2/λmax of the Hessian). In a deep network, backpropagation is the chain-rule bookkeeping that gets this gradient for every weight in one backward pass. Optimizers like Adam add momentum and per-parameter step sizes on top.

Chapter 3 · How LLMs work

How a large language model turns a question into an answer

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot all run on large language models, or LLMs. Under the conversation is one repeated act: predicting the next piece of text.

Read the transcript

Here's how a large language model is built, in about ninety seconds. Start with the core trick. The model reads some text and predicts the next small piece of it, called a token. That's it. Do that billions of times across a huge amount of text, and the model ends up absorbing grammar, facts, and patterns of reasoning, because all of those help it predict better. Inside, every word is turned into a list of numbers, so that words with similar meanings end up close together. A mechanism called attention lets each word look at the other words around it. That's how the model can tell a river bank from a savings bank. This first stage is called pretraining, and it produces a model that's good at continuing text but not at being helpful. Next comes fine-tuning, where the model learns from examples of good questions and good answers. Then a third stage uses human feedback. People compare answers, a separate reward model learns their preferences, and the main model is adjusted toward answers people rate as better. Some labs also train against a written set of principles, called a constitution. One honest caveat: none of this gives the model perfect memory or guaranteed truth. Its context window is finite, and it can still state wrong things confidently.

Step 1: Predict the next token

An LLM reads text as tokens, which are whole words or pieces of words. For each position, it produces a score for every token it knows and turns those scores into probabilities. Then one token gets picked, added to the text, and the whole process runs again. A full answer is that loop, repeated a few hundred times.

These are the model's guesses for the next word. Press Pick next word to draw one by its probability. Then move the temperature slider: low makes the model play it safe, high makes it take chances.

Real models choose from vocabularies of tens of thousands of tokens, and the probabilities come from billions of learned numbers. The probabilities above are made up to show the mechanism.

Builder

The bars are a softmax over logits z scaled by temperature T: pi = ezi/T / Σ ezj/T. As T → 0 this approaches greedy decoding. Production samplers usually add top-p or top-k truncation. Tokenizers are typically byte-pair encodings, so "windowsill" may be one token or two ("window" + "sill") depending on the vocabulary.

Step 2: Meaning as numbers

To predict well, the model needs some sense of what words mean. It gets that by turning each token into a long list of numbers called an embedding. During training, tokens used in similar ways drift toward similar numbers. So "puppy" ends up near "dog," and "loan" near "deposit." Think of it as a map where distance stands for difference in meaning.

Step 3: Attention, or reading words in context

"Bank" sits between two clusters on that map because, on its own, it's ambiguous. What settles it is the rest of the sentence. The breakthrough behind today's models, the transformer, introduced in the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need, lets every token look at every other token in the text and pull in meaning from the ones that matter. That mechanism is called self-attention.

Interactive · AttentionIllustrative weights

How "bank" decides what it means

Switch sentences, then tap any word to see which other words it pays most attention to. Darker means more attention.

A real model runs many attention "heads" in parallel across dozens of layers, each picking up different relationships. The weights shown are invented to illustrate one plausible pattern.

Practitioner

Before transformers, the leading language models were recurrent networks, which read one word at a time and carried a running summary forward. That made them slow to train and prone to losing track of earlier text. Attention looks at the whole input at once, which suits parallel chips (GPUs) and is a big part of why models could grow so large. The cost is that attention's work grows with the square of the input length. That's one reason every model has a fixed context window.

Common misreadings
  • "Attention gives the model perfect memory." No. The context window is finite, and models can still overlook or mix up details inside it.
  • "Transformers scale forever." Bigger models have reliably done better so far, but that's an observed trend, not a guarantee.
  • "Scaling laws are proven math." They're empirical: curves fitted to experiments, which researchers revise as new results come in.
  • "Feedback training makes the model truthful." It makes answers people prefer more likely. Fluent and wrong is still possible, which is why checking matters (Chapter 4).

Chapter 4 · Using AI

Using AI well: say what you need, and check what you get

For most people, this is the branch that pays off first. Two habits make most of the difference: tell the tool what you actually need, and verify what comes back.

A prompt isn't a magic phrase. It's a work order. The model can't see your situation, your audience, or what "good" looks like to you, so it guesses. Every useful thing you add to the prompt means one less guess. Build one below.

Judging what comes back

        
LLMs hallucinate: they produce fluent, confident text that's wrong. That includes citations to court cases, studies, and statutes that don't exist. It follows from how they work: the model predicts likely text, and a likely-looking citation isn't necessarily a real one. The more the stakes depend on a fact, a number, or a source, the more it needs checking against the original.

        
The other habit is knowing what not to paste in. Whether something is safe to share depends on the tool and on your organization's rules. A business account with a data agreement is different from a free consumer app. Try sorting these.

Chapter 5 · Building with AI

        
Building with AI: give the model a library and tools

        
A model alone only knows what it absorbed in training, up to a cutoff date. Most real AI applications get around this by handing the model fresh information, and sometimes tools, at the moment it answers.

        
The most common pattern is retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. You split your documents into short passages and store each one with its embedding (Chapter 3's "meaning as numbers"). When a question comes in, the system finds the passages closest in meaning, hands them to the model, and asks it to answer from those, with citations. Agents: models that take actions

        
An agent is a model in a loop with tools. It reads a goal, decides on a step (search this, run that, open a file), sees the result, and decides the next step, until the job is done or it stops to ask. Coding assistants that edit and test software work this way. The loop multiplies what a model can do, and it multiplies what can go wrong. That's why the security questions in Chapter 7 now apply to agents in particular.

        

          Practitioner
          
Before buying or building, decide which of three tools you need. Prompting changes behavior. Retrieval adds knowledge. Fine-tuning changes style or specializes a smaller model. Most organizations need retrieval far more often than fine-tuning. Then ask how the system is evaluated: a fixed set of real questions with known good answers, run on every change, is the minimum.

          
Short courses: DeepLearning.AI (RAG, agents, evals) and the Hugging Face Agents Course. For a plain overview of patterns, see Anthropic's Building Effective Agents.

        

        

          Builder
          
The Navigator's stack, for reference: about 180 chunks from about 50 SC sources, embedded with Voyage (1,024 dimensions) in a Pinecone serverless index, retrieved with a cap of 3 chunks per source so one long document can't crowd out the rest, then answered by Claude with citations. Each bill is indexed as its own record for the same reason. Tool access across apps is increasingly standardized through the Model Context Protocol. Further reading: the original RAG paper (Lewis et al., 2020), Chip Huyen's AI Engineering, and Hamel Husain on evals.

Chips, data centers, and power lines

Who pays for the power? A large AI data center can draw as much electricity as a small city. When a utility has to build new generation and new transmission lines to serve that kind of customer, someone has to pay for them. Without a deliberate choice, those costs tend to spread across everyone's bill, including households. In April 2025, Santee Cooper, South Carolina's state-owned utility, voted on a different approach: an experimental four-year rate structure for customers that draw fifty megawatts or more. The idea is to line up the cost of new infrastructure with the customers driving the demand for it. Other states have gone the opposite way, offering discounts to attract investment. Reasonable people disagree about which approach is better, and the answer depends on local conditions. What's distinctive about South Carolina is that it has asked the question out loud. That question increasingly lands in front of county councils, which decide on siting and incentive packages.

        


        
The most distinctive thing about how South Carolina is hosting the AI build-out is not that it is hosting it. It is that the state has chosen to ask out loud who is paying for it.


        

          Practitioner
          
Water is the other local question. Many data centers cool with water, and some use far more than others depending on design. Ask for site-specific figures rather than national averages. For the national picture, see the IEA's Energy and AI report and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's 2024 U.S. Data Center Energy Usage Report.           Builder
          
Training and inference stress hardware differently. Training is one enormous, tightly networked job that runs for weeks. Inference is many small jobs where memory bandwidth and latency dominate. Inference is a growing share of total demand as usage grows. That's why "how big is the model" no longer predicts "how much power" on its own. Industry depth: SemiAnalysis (partly paywalled). Chapter 7 · Safety and governance

        
Risk and governance: the right tool for the right problem

        
"Is AI dangerous?" has no useful answer, because AI risk isn't one thing. SCAIO's working map sorts any risk by asking where the harm comes from and how far off it is.

        
    
          
  • Misuse: people deliberately using AI to do harm. The response is mostly law enforcement and access controls.
    • 
          
  • Malfunction: the system does something nobody intended. The response is testing, evaluation, and human oversight.
    • 
          
  • Systemic: harms that emerge from widespread adoption itself. The response is policy and institutional design.
    • 
        

        
Each can be here now, emerging, or over the horizon. Try placing these.

Is AI dangerous? That question has no useful answer, because AI risk isn't one thing. SCAIO's map sorts any risk with two questions. First, where does the harm come from? Misuse is people deliberately using AI to do harm, like a scammer cloning someone's voice. Malfunction is a system doing something nobody intended, like a hiring tool that quietly screens out qualified people. Systemic risk comes from widespread adoption itself, like disruption to jobs. Second, how far off is it: here now, emerging, or over the horizon? The answers point to different responses. Misuse is mostly a job for law enforcement. Malfunction calls for testing and human oversight. Systemic risk is a question for policy. South Carolina has already acted on misuse. In 2025 it made AI-generated child sexual abuse material a crime, along with distributing intimate images, real or AI-made, without consent. Matching the response to the source is most of the work.

Key takeaways

        
    
          
  • A language model predicts the next token. Embeddings and attention are how it gets meaning and context, and three training stages turn it into an assistant.
    • 
          
  • Fluent isn't the same as correct. Tell the tool what you need, check what it gives you, and keep sensitive data out of tools that aren't approved for it.
    • 
          
  • Most useful AI systems hand the model sources (retrieval) or tools (agents). Evaluate them on real questions.
    • 
          
  • AI is physical. Chips, data centers, power, and water are where South Carolina's biggest AI decisions are being made right now.
    • 
          
  • Sort a risk before arguing about it. Misuse, malfunction, and systemic harms need different responses.
