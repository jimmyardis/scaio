Why a separate primer

Most AI advice is written for the people who run systems. You vote on them.

South Carolina already has a SCAIO primer on AI for local government. It is written for administrators, IT directors, and clerks — the people who select tools, configure them, and defend the network.

A county council member's job is different. You rarely choose the software. You approve the budget that buys it, the rezoning that hosts it, the incentive agreement that finances it, and the policies that govern it. You are accountable to residents for all four, usually in public, often with incomplete information, and frequently on a timeline set by someone else.

This primer covers the AI decisions that arrive on a county council agenda — and what to ask when they do.

01 · Land, power, and incentives

Hosting the build-out

Rezonings, siting, infrastructure commitments, and fee-in-lieu-of-tax agreements for data centers. The largest dollar figures a council will see, and the decisions hardest to reverse.

02 · The county's own use

Budget and procurement approval

AI tools bought for county departments — drafting, permitting, call handling, assessment. You approve the spending and, in practice, set the policy floor for how it is used.

Public records, open-meeting materials, and what residents are entitled to know about how decisions affecting them were made.

A worked example

The Spartanburg package, and why the numbers deserve a hard look.

SC Daily Gazette reported in March 2025 on "Moc-1," a proposed $2.8 billion computing center in Spartanburg County. According to that reporting, the facility would generate its own electricity on site, would serve the company's own internal operations rather than lease capacity, and is expected to employ 27 full-time staff.

The county's offer, as reported: a reduced 4% property tax rate for 40 years against the standard 6%, plus tax credits of roughly $1.5 million annually for the first 20 years.

None of that makes the deal wrong. On-site generation genuinely avoids the grid-impact questions other projects raise, and a large capital investment can carry a county's tax base for decades. But a 40-year commitment at 27 jobs is a different proposition from a 40-year commitment at 700 jobs, and it should be evaluated as the proposition it actually is.

Without an explicit price-signal mechanism, the cost of meeting that growth tends to flow through to ratepayers broadly — meaning the residential customer in a 1,200-square-foot home in Rock Hill helps subsidize the cost of grid expansion driven by a hyperscaler facility that uses more power in an hour than the home does in two years."

From SCAIO's Journal article, "South Carolina's Data-Center Decade"
Who pays for the power

South Carolina has started answering that question out loud.

In April 2025, SC Daily Gazette reported that Santee Cooper voted to enact an experimental electricity rate structure for customers drawing 50 megawatts or more — a four-year pilot designed so that very large new loads contribute to the cost of new generating capacity rather than having those costs spread across existing residential and small-business customers.

The 50 MW threshold captures essentially every modern AI-focused data center campus, and several large EV-and-battery plants besides.

This is genuinely contested ground, and reasonable people land in different places. Some peer states subsidize large loads rather than surcharging them, betting that long-run economic benefit exceeds short-run ratepayer cost. Both arguments have merit. What matters for a council is knowing which bet your county is making, and on whose behalf.

Before you vote on a siting or incentive package

Six questions worth asking on the record.

Q1

What is the jobs-per-dollar-of-incentive figure, and how does it compare to our other agreements?

Ask for it in writing. It is the single clearest way to compare an AI-infrastructure package against the industrial deals a county already knows how to evaluate.

Q2

Where does the electricity come from, and who pays for any new capacity?

On-site generation, a dedicated renewable facility, and ordinary grid draw are three very different answers with three very different bills attached.

Q3

How much water does it use, from where, and what happens in a drought year?

Cooling water is the least-discussed input in most siting conversations and among the most locally consequential.

Before you vote, continued

The three that are easiest to forget.

Q4

What are the clawback terms if the jobs or investment do not materialize?

Every incentive agreement should answer this. If the draft in front of you does not, that is the amendment to ask for.

Q5

What does this commit the county to in years 20 through 40?

Most council members voting on a 40-year agreement will not be in office for the back half of it. Someone should still be able to describe what it obligates.

Q6

What did we agree to keep confidential, and for how long?

Non-disclosure is normal in economic development. It is still a decision, and residents are entitled to know its shape even when they cannot know its contents.

Inside the county

Your own departments are already using AI.

Whether or not a county has an AI policy, staff are using AI tools — drafting correspondence, summarizing long documents, preparing meeting materials. This is mostly good, and the productivity gains are real. It is also happening whether or not anyone has written down the rules.

Councils do not need to become technologists to govern this well. Two decisions cover most of it: what categories of county data may be put into an outside AI service, and which decisions affecting residents require a named human being accountable for the outcome.

For the procurement side of this, see SCAIO's primer on AI procurement for SC government.

The public record

Three transparency questions specific to AI.

  • Is AI-assisted work product subject to FOIA in the same way other work product is? Assume yes, and make sure staff assume it too.
  • If an AI system helps make a determination affecting a resident — an assessment, a permit, an eligibility decision — can the county explain that determination to the person it affected?
  • Does anything in the county's AI use create a record residents cannot see but the vendor can? Prompts, uploaded documents, and query logs may live on a vendor's systems under vendor terms.
